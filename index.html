The idea

An array fingertip that gives
differential force distributions.

Four curved beams make a 30 mm fingertip, holding a wine glass, and sensitive enough to feel the interaction with the environment.

Each beam carries a printed strain gauge. Different loads bend the four beams in different patterns, so four readings reveal normal and shear forces, bending and twisting torques, and the related poses in a differential way. The fingertips plug onto a commercial gripper in place of its original tips.

01 / Why sense with compliance

Soft enough to interact,
sensitive enough to notice.

Why put compliance and sensing in the same fingertip?

Compliance keeps fragile objects from being crushed; sensing lets a robot grasp safely and correct itself. Camera-based tactile fingertips reconstruct contact in fine detail, but they need a camera and lighting inside the finger, and forces usually come from a learned model. Dense tactile arrays measure force more directly, yet they are costly to make, slow to sample, and need extensive electronics.

This fingertip takes a simpler route: the beams that bend are also the sensors.

Four differential readings are few, yet enough for sensing.

The four kinds of load, with the fingertips on a commercial OnRobot gripper. Photographs from Fig. 1(b).

02 / Mechanical design

Four curved beams,
one fingertip.

How does a 30 mm fingertip feel a push from the environment?

The fingertip is a 2 × 2 array of contact regions, each formed by one convex curved beam printed in PLA, 9.8 mm wide with a 31.12 mm curve length. A beam is pinned at one end by a revolute joint and slides at the other through a prismatic joint, so it flexes on contact instead of resisting rigidly. The pinned ends sit in the middle of the base and the sliding ends at the outer sides; a thin silica-gel pad adds grip.

Because the beams sit in different places, each load bends them in its own pattern. A normal push raises all four readings. Under a shear force, the readings of the two pairs of beams change in opposite directions. Bending and twisting torques press one side harder than the other, pairing the beams along different axes.

03 / A printed strain gauge

A cheap gauge
that lasts.

Can a flexible-circuit strain gauge survive 200,000 bends?

Each beam carries a full-bridge strain gauge etched in copper on a flexible polyimide circuit, using the same process as an ordinary circuit board. Its 2 × 2 grid responds to local bending, cancels temperature changes, and conforms to the curved beam. A 38 × 28 × 20 mm³ board amplifies the signals and sends them over Bluetooth Low Energy, sampling all four gauges at over 1 kHz.

0.4%Reading fluctuation, 10 mV of 2,500 mV
≈ 32 mVLargest hysteresis in one cycle
8.3%Drift after 200,000 cycles (77 mV)

Over 200,000 loading cycles the readings stayed steady. The largest drift at the extreme positions was 77 mV, 8.3% of the reading range, which the authors suggest may come from plastic deformation of the beam.

04 / Validation

Pushes, slips
and twists.

What can four strain readings tell you?

One fingertip was loaded through a plate mounted on a commercial force/torque sensor and moved by translational and rotational slides, accurate to 0.1 mm and 0.02°. The readings streamed at 100 Hz to match the reference sensor and were compared with the applied loads.

Pressing the plate down in 2 mm steps raised all four readings. The first 0.5 N already moved the readings by 14 mV, twice their fluctuation, so the resolution near first contact is better than 0.5 N; with friction it was below 0.3 N.

Sliding the pressed plate sideways in 1 mm steps changed the readings of the two pairs of beams in opposite directions. When all four readings jumped together instead, the fingertip had slipped, so the same signals that measure shear also detect slip. The clearest step was a 3.37 N increase, read as a 9 mV change.

Tilting the plate from −10° to 10° pressed one side of the fingertip harder than the other, and that side's readings changed most. A refined test from 0° to −2° resolved 0.063 N·m, a 2° change in angle.

Twisting paired the beams diagonally, #1 with #3 and #2 with #4. The smallest resolved step was 0.042 N·m, a 4° change in angle.

05 / On a commercial gripper

Application:
hanging a wine glass.

Can a gripper notice a bad grasp, a push, or a bump?

Two fingertips replaced the original tips of an OnRobot gripper on a collaborative arm. Their eight readings streamed wirelessly at 50 Hz while the robot picked up a glass holding a 200 g weight, flipped it, and hung it on a rack, in nine stages.

All nine stages, photographed during the experiment (Fig. 10b). Scroll sideways to see every stage.

When the glass first met the rack in stage VII, the right fingertip's readings dropped by 6 mV as the glass pressed toward the left fingertip, and opposite changes across its beams showed a bending torque: the glass was misaligned with the rack opening. The robot backed off, realigned, and hung the glass.

Questions & answers

Common questions.

Does the fingertip output newtons yet?

Not yet. The paper reports raw readings next to reference loads and shows that each kind of load changes them in a distinct, repeatable way. Turning readings into forces, torques and poses needs calibration, which the authors list as future work.

How fast is it?

The board can sample all four gauges at over 1 kHz. The bench tests streamed at 100 Hz to match the reference force/torque sensor, and the gripper demonstration at 50 Hz.

How durable is the sensor?

In a 200,000-cycle bending test, readings at the extreme positions drifted by at most 77 mV, 8.3% of the range, with about 32 mV of hysteresis within a cycle.

Can it detect slip?

Yes. Under shear, the readings of the two pairs of beams normally change in opposite directions; when all four readings jump together, the contact has slipped. In the gripper demonstration, a slip showed up as readings that did not return to their earlier values after the glass was pushed.

What would improve it?

The authors suggest metal beams such as spring steel for more precise shapes, a sampling circuit built into the fingertip, optimized beam curves, and larger arrays such as 3 × 3.
